Ethics and Privacy Reflection

The ethical and privacy frameworks covered in this course, applied to the design of this application — including where it falls short.

Ethics in a professional IT context

Ethics is the reasoning used to work out what is right and wrong when rules, laws and instinct do not settle the question on their own. It matters particularly in IT because the people affected by a system usually cannot see how it works. A student using this directory cannot tell from the outside whether it records who viewed what, so the decision not to record it is invisible to them and has to be taken on their behalf.

That gap between what a system does and what its users can see is why the profession publishes codes. The example given in this course is the Australian Computer Society, which maintains both a Code of Professional Ethics and a Code of Professional Conduct. The first sets out the values an ACS member commits to — the primacy of the public interest, enhancement of quality of life, honesty, competence, professional development and professionalism — and the second describes what following those values looks like in practice.

The clause that bears hardest on this project is the primacy of the public interest: it ranks the interests of the people affected above personal or sectional ones. The honest-limitations section near the bottom of this page is that clause applied. Describing a client-side login as real security would breach the code more seriously than writing a weak login did in the first place, because the weak login is a constraint of the assignment while the overclaim would be a choice to mislead.

What privacy is

Privacy is commonly viewed as a right to having “an inviolate personality” (Warren & Brandeis 1890, p. 216). The thing being protected is human dignity — the ability to be free from authoritative control and intrusion — and that is distinct from the protection of property or of free expression (Floridi 2016; Mill 1885). The argument rests on humans being distinct in that we are “endowed with consciousness, intelligence, mental life and self-determination” (Floridi 2016).

Reiman makes the link to personhood directly: the right to privacy “protects the individual’s interest in becoming, being and remaining a person” (Reiman 1976, p. 44). On that reading we should be able to manage the details and the nature of the information communicated about us, which is a matter of confidentiality and of trust in how data is disclosed.

Read that way, privacy is not mainly about secrecy. A student advertising a volleyball social is not keeping a secret; they are managing what is communicated about them. A system that quietly widened that communication — by also recording who read the listing — would take the management away from them without ever revealing a secret.

The four privacy theories

The course sets out four theories of privacy, and they disagree about what the right actually protects.

  • Control theory links privacy with freedom, on the basis that people should be in control of their own information (Westin 1967).
  • Restricted access theory is concerned with access to personal data being limited, because data is shared at a pace users cannot control and manage (Moor 1990).
  • Ontological theory holds that privacy is the friction provided by technologies in the infosphere (Floridi 2006).
  • Contextual integrity holds that “we have a right to privacy, but it is neither a right to control personal information nor a right to have access to this information restricted, but it is a right to appropriate flow of personal information” (Nissenbaum 2010, p. 127).

Which theory fits this application

An events directory looks harmless. It lists public activities and does not ask anyone for a bank account or a medical record. But knowing which events a student recommends tells you something about their interests, their religion or culture, their political views, their disability, or their sexuality. A directory that recorded who viewed or recommended a Pride society event, a prayer group, or a disability support workshop would be holding genuinely sensitive information about identifiable students, even though every individual event in the list is public.

Contextual integrity is the theory that explains why that is a privacy problem at all. Control theory struggles here, because nobody has lost control of a fact they chose to publish, and restricted access struggles for the same reason, because the information was never restricted. Nissenbaum is the one that fits: a poster for a prayer group on a noticeboard is an appropriate flow in that context, and the same event in a searchable system that also logged who viewed it is an inappropriate flow. The harm is in the change of context, not in the disclosure of a secret.

The other three still do work. Restricted access explains the pace problem — a student who posts one event has no sense of how far a searchable, indexed listing travels compared with a paper poster. Floridi’s ontological theory describes what removing the organiser email field actually did: it put friction back into the system deliberately, by making the easy path — mass-contacting every organiser — impossible rather than merely discouraged.

IT governance and its mechanisms

IT governance is a “framework for the leadership, organizational structures and business processes, standards and compliance to these standards, which ensure that the organization’s IT supports and enables the achievement of its strategies and objectives” (Calder 2007), or more briefly, “the processes that ensure the effective and efficient use of IT in enabling an organization to achieve its goals” (Gartner 2023). It covers four major areas (Ebert et al. 2020, p. 13):

  • Strategic alignment — connecting IT with business strategies and operations.
  • Value delivery — ensuring the technology generates the expected benefits.
  • Resource management — optimising IT investment and managing applications, information, infrastructure and people.
  • Performance measurement — monitoring the implementation of the strategy and the performance of the processes adopted.

Established frameworks for doing this include ITIL and COBIT. Neither is proportionate to a one-person student project, but the function they perform is not optional, and this project has lightweight equivalents of it: the three-week sprint plan on the troubleshooting blog is the strategic alignment and performance measurement, and the git history is the audit record that shows when each decision was actually made.

Governance mechanisms

Governance is enforced through three kinds of mechanism.

  • Codes of ethics — the ACS Code of Professional Ethics and Code of Professional Conduct, the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct, and the IFIP Code of Ethics.
  • Laws — on data protection, such as the GDPR and Consumer Data Rights; on privacy, such as the Australian Privacy Principles and the Privacy Act, which is currently undergoing reform; and on intellectual property.
  • Practices — for privacy, confidentiality, disclosure, impact risk assessments and notifiable data breaches; for intellectual property, trade marks, patents and design rights; Creative Commons licences and attributions; and security measures.

Which of these this application would answer to

  • Australian Privacy Principles. APP 3 limits collection to what is reasonably necessary; this application satisfies it by collecting no personal data at all. APP 5 requires notice of what is collected and why, and the site has no privacy notice anywhere, so it would fail APP 5 — which is why a visible privacy notice is first on the list of things I would add. APP 11 requires reasonable security, which the limitations section below addresses honestly.
  • Notifiable data breaches. The scheme requires an organisation to notify individuals of a breach likely to cause serious harm. Collecting no personal data is what makes this obligation almost empty here: there is nothing held whose loss would need reporting. That is data minimisation paying off twice.
  • GDPR. It does not apply to a campus assignment, but its requirements for a lawful basis, data minimisation and a right to erasure are the standard the design was measured against. Erasure in particular is satisfied in an unusual way: the data sits in the student’s own browser, so they can clear it themselves without asking anyone.
  • Creative Commons and attribution. Every image on this site is an SVG written by hand as part of the project, so nothing is used under a licence that requires attribution and nothing has been taken without one. Had stock photography been used, each licence and attribution would have needed to be recorded.

The six ethical lenses

When facing a difficult decision it is not just about following rules, laws or emotions. The six lenses created by the Markkula Center for Applied Ethics each provide a distinct framework for ethical reasoning, and examining a problem through all six gives a more rounded view than any one of them does alone.

  • The Right Lens — respect everyone’s basic rights, such as truth, privacy and the freedom to shape their own life choices. Does this decision honour and protect everyone’s rights?
  • The Justice Lens — fairness: people receiving what they are rightfully due, through equal treatment or fair distinction based on need or merit, in its social, corrective, retributive and restorative forms. Does this decision uphold fairness for everyone involved?
  • The Utilitarian Lens — consequences: maximise benefit and minimise harm. Which decision helps the most people while causing the least harm?
  • The Common Good Lens — the well-being of the entire community rather than individual needs alone. Does this action contribute to a stronger, healthier community for everyone?
  • The Virtue Lens — character and growth: becoming a good person, not only making the right call in the moment. How is this action shaping the person I am becoming?
  • The Care Ethics Lens — relationships and empathy, understanding people’s emotions and unique circumstances. Am I demonstrating empathy and consideration for those affected?

No single lens provides all the answers, and at times they conflict. That is the point of having six: the conflict is information, not a problem to be tidied away.

The lenses applied to one real decision

Rather than run all six over every feature, I applied them to the decision that was a genuine trade-off: removing the organiser contact email field from the create-event form.

  • Right — students have a right to decide who can contact them. Storing addresses takes that decision away from an organiser who only wanted a listing.
  • Justice — the same rule applies to every society: no group gets its contact details published and no group is left out.
  • Utilitarian — the field saves a few students a search; a leak exposes every organiser. Small benefit spread thin, large harm concentrated.
  • Common good — a directory students trust is worth more to everyone than one that is marginally more convenient but collects more than it needs.
  • Virtue — I could explain “we did not collect it” to a room of students. I could not comfortably explain “we collected it in case it turned out to be useful”.
  • Care — not the average user, but the student whose event is a Pride society meet-up or a prayer group, for whom being on a contactable list is a different proposition.

Where the lenses disagree

The audit trail is the case where they pull in opposite directions. Justice and the common good argue for logging who deleted an event, because accountability is a form of fairness. The right and care lenses argue against it, because an audit log is a standing record of identifiable activity. The answer is not to pick a favourite lens but to look for the option that survives most of them — which is why the resolution below logs administrator actions only and leaves ordinary browsing unlogged.

The five-step decision process, applied

The course sets out a five-step process for working through an ethical decision. Run over the email field decision, it looks like this.

  1. Identify the ethical issue. Could this cause harm, or benefit some people more than others? A stored list of organiser addresses benefits the application and carries the risk for the organisers, so the people taking the risk are not the people getting the benefit. That split is what makes it ethical rather than merely technical.
  2. Gather the facts. Affected parties are the organisers whose addresses would be stored, the students who would use them, and the university whose name is on the directory. The options were: collect and display the address, collect it but hide it behind the login, or not collect it and let descriptions say “contact the society”.
  3. Evaluate the options using the lenses. The six-lens run above. All six point the same way, which is the signal that the decision is not finely balanced.
  4. Choose the action and test it. Would I be comfortable explaining this publicly? “We decided not to hold your email address” is a comfortable sentence to say to a room of student organisers. “We held it because it might be useful later” is not, and that discomfort is the test doing its job.
  5. Reflect on the outcome. The cost was real: students cannot contact an organiser directly from the listing. The follow-up is to make the notes field carry that information in a form the organiser chooses to publish, which keeps the decision with the person it belongs to. Reflect, refine, repeat.

Features that support ethical data use

1. Data minimisation — the application collects no personal data at all

The strongest privacy measure available was to not collect the data in the first place. Every field on the create-event form describes an event: title, course, category, date, time, mode, location, description and notes. There is no field for the organiser’s name, no email address, no phone number, no student ID, and no attendee list.

This was a deliberate choice rather than an oversight. An earlier version of my field list included an “organiser contact email” so that students could get in touch about an event. I removed it, because storing a list of contactable addresses turns a harmless directory into a target worth attacking, and because a description can simply say “contact the society” without the system holding the address itself. Collecting less data means there is less to lose, less to secure, and less to misuse.

2. No tracking of who looks at what

The application records which events exist. It does not record who searched for what, which events were viewed, how long anyone spent reading a description, or which categories a user filters by most often. All of that would be easy to add and commercially tempting, and all of it would build a profile of a student’s interests without their knowledge.

There are no analytics scripts, no third-party trackers, no advertising pixels and no cookies. Every file the site loads comes from within the project folder, which you can verify by opening the browser’s Network tab: every request is to a local file.

3. Honest, non-manipulative interface design

Deleting an event triggers a confirmation dialog that names the event and states plainly that the action cannot be undone, and Cancel is a real option rather than a greyed-out discouragement. This is the opposite of a dark pattern — an interface deliberately designed to push a user into the choice that serves the system rather than the user. Similarly, a recommendation is an explicit button press that can be pressed again to undo, so nothing is recommended on a student’s behalf from inferred behaviour.

4. Accessibility as an ethical obligation

An application that excludes disabled students is not ethically neutral. Each image carries descriptive alt text; form fields are joined to their labels with for and id; error messages are marked aria-invalid and announced through role="alert"; the keyboard focus outline is deliberately kept visible rather than removed for appearance; and colour is never the only signal — a recommended event is marked by the word “Recommended” as well as by an amber badge, so it is still identifiable to a colour-blind user.

Features that support data privacy

1. Access control on the page that can change data

The events directory is behind a login, so the create, update and delete controls are not available to an anonymous visitor. This is the principle of least privilege: the ability to change or destroy data is restricted to the role that needs it.

The failure message is part of the design too. When a sign-in fails the application says “Incorrect username or password” rather than “No such user”. A message naming which half was wrong would confirm to an attacker which usernames exist, letting them build a list of valid accounts before they start guessing passwords. This is called username enumeration, and avoiding it costs nothing.

2. The session ends when the browser closes

The signed-in state is held in sessionStorage, not localStorage. localStorage survives until explicitly cleared, so a student who signed in on a library computer would still be signed in for the next person who sat down; sessionStorage is discarded when the tab closes. Only the username is stored — the password is compared and then discarded, so it cannot be recovered afterwards by inspecting the browser — and a Sign out button lets a user end the session deliberately.

3. Input validation, which protects the system as well as the data

Every field is validated before anything is saved: required fields must not be empty, text fields have minimum lengths, times must be real 24-hour times, and dates must be real calendar dates — a date such as 30 February has the right shape but does not exist, and is rejected by rebuilding the date and checking the browser did not silently roll it over into March. Dates more than five years away are rejected as probable typing errors. Validation is a privacy measure and not only a tidiness measure: unvalidated input is the entry point for most common web attacks.

4. Protection against cross-site scripting

This is the most important technical privacy control in the application. Every piece of event text is placed on the page using textContent, never innerHTML:

// js/events.js - how every piece of user text reaches the page
function el(tag, className, text) {
    const node = document.createElement(tag);
    if (className) node.className = className;
    if (text !== undefined) node.textContent = text;   // text, never markup
    return node;
}

The difference is critical. innerHTML parses the string it is given as HTML, so an event title of <img src=x onerror=alert(1)> would be turned into a real element and the JavaScript inside it would run. textContent writes the string as literal characters, so the same input is displayed as the text a user typed and nothing executes. This is a cross-site scripting (XSS) defence, and it matters here because the application displays text that one user typed to a different user — exactly the situation XSS exploits. A successful XSS attack on a real deployment could read another student’s session and act as them.

I verified this works by entering <img src=x onerror=alert(1)> as an event title and confirming it appeared on screen as those literal characters, with no image element created and no script run.

5. The data never leaves the student’s own device

Events are stored in the browser’s localStorage on the machine where they were entered. There is no server, no database and no network request, so nothing a user types is transmitted anywhere. For a directory of public campus events this is a reasonable architecture, and it means a student can clear everything the application knows about them using their own browser settings, without asking anyone’s permission.

Honest limitations of this implementation

Claiming this application is secure would itself be an ethical failure, so these are the places where the implementation is weaker than it may appear.

The login is not real security

The username and password are written in js/auth.js, which the browser downloads in full, so anyone can read them from the page source or the developer tools. Real authentication cannot be done in the browser alone, because any check that runs on the user’s machine can be bypassed by the user. It requires a server that stores a hash of the password produced by a slow algorithm designed for the purpose, such as bcrypt or Argon2 — never the password itself — compares a submitted password against that hash, and returns a signed session token, leaving the browser only to carry the token. Since this assignment is a client-side application with no server, the login here is the honest limit of what is achievable, and I would not deploy it to protect anything that mattered.

localStorage is not encrypted or protected

Data in localStorage is stored as plain text and is readable by anyone with access to the computer, and by any JavaScript running on the same origin. It is appropriate for public event listings and would not be appropriate for anything private.

There is no audit trail

Because no identity is recorded against an action, the system cannot tell who deleted an event. There is a genuine tension here: an audit log would improve accountability, but it would also mean recording identifiable activity for every user, which is exactly what data minimisation avoids. A production system would resolve this by logging administrator actions, where accountability outweighs the privacy cost, while continuing not to log ordinary browsing.

What I would add with more time

  • A visible privacy notice stating what is stored and for how long, which is the APP 5 gap identified above.
  • A server with a database, properly hashed passwords and session tokens.
  • HTTPS, so data in transit cannot be read on a shared campus network.
  • A retention rule that removes events a set period after they finish, so the directory does not accumulate data indefinitely.
  • Rate limiting on the login form to slow down password guessing.

Summary

Most privacy protection is decided at design time, not added afterwards. The decision not to collect organiser email addresses protects more students than any security measure added later, because data that was never collected cannot be breached — and all six lenses agreed on it. Equally, the choice between textContent and innerHTML is a single word in a single function, and it is the difference between an application that is safe to display other people’s text in and one that is not. Being clear about what this application does not protect is part of the same responsibility.
